# D3 selected hosting: one Fly Machine with Neon Free Postgres

27 September 2026. **Selected hosting, partly provisioned, not live-proven.** The owner chose the lowest-cost Fly plus Neon Free path and accepted Free's shorter point-in-time restore history instead of paying for Neon Launch's seven-day window. This document supersedes the [reviewed Launch design](hosted-fly-neon-launch.md) for plan, cost, quota, recovery-window and acceptance statements. Its unchanged team authority, data classes, effect boundaries, live-root pin, external recovery manifest, physical/provider fence and staged S04/S05/S29-S31 controls carry forward. The [earlier Fly Managed Postgres design](hosted-deployment-options.md) remains historical. The [D2 durable-attempt contract](hosted-work-contract.md), 20-file Story limit and approved 90-day minimal post-terminal metadata lifetime remain unchanged. The owner split S02 into S02a and S02b, giving [six ordered product outcomes](../delivery-plan.md). The [D3 first-service Task](s02a-first-service.md) proposes starting the service secret holder no later than S02a; its 22–26-file combined estimate requires re-grooming before coding and may add a complete prerequisite PR. The exact changed design and plan need independent review; earlier CLEAN verdicts remain tied to their recorded artifacts.

## Outcome and current facts

The one-team pilot should inspect an existing Cursor run, validate its result, explicitly launch at most one eligible run, and publish safely from an always-available shared coordinator. Its run and effect state must survive app restarts and be visible to another authorized team member. A Fly `pigboard` organization exists but has no app or database. A separate Pigboard Neon Free organization and Frankfurt project now exist; owner-console persistence and idle wake have narrow proof. A scoped application role, ordinary-child-target denial, app-client reconnect and Fly use remain unverified. The existing Entendia and Joseph organizations and all their projects are excluded from Pigboard work.

The selected starting Fly size is **shared-cpu-1x with 1 GB RAM**, subject to a one-team load/memory check. [Fly's October pricing update](https://fly.io/pricing-update/) lists $2.19/month for shared-cpu-1x/256 MB and $6 per added GB-month of RAM, implying about **$6.69/month** for 1 GB after 1 October, subject to actual region/configuration and network usage. The signed-in Neon organization form offers Free at **$0/month with no credit card**, 100 compute unit (CU) hours and 0.5 GB of Postgres storage per project. [Neon's current Free-plan announcement](https://neon.com/blog/neon-backend-is-ga) also lists 10 branches per project; [Neon's recovery explanation](https://neon.com/blog/new-usage-based-pricing) describes Free point restores as up to six hours or 1 GB of changes, whichever is smaller. Configure one root compute at fixed 0.25 CU with scale-to-zero. A project running continuously at 0.25 CU would use about 182.5 CU-hours in a 730-hour month and exceed Free's 100 CU-hour allowance; the mathematical ceiling is 400 active hours at that size, including recovery-branch compute. The pilot is viable on Free only if measured use, storage, branch count and transfer stay within the actual account limits. Fly's estimate is not a hard spending cap. Do not create or upgrade to a paid Neon plan automatically. If a Free limit blocks work, stop new effects, report the observed quota and obtain a new plan decision.

## Interfaces, failure and recovery

The app connects to Neon over a TLS-verified public endpoint in the same European region. Hosted connection strings live only in Fly app secrets. The first shared S02a command runs through the Fly-held service boundary, potentially delivered by a complete prerequisite technical Story before S02a; local human and agent callers use authenticated service commands rather than direct database credentials. No database URL or caller grant belongs in a workflow profile, ticket or repository. Before the first S02a shared-store use, a reviewed, non-secret configuration pins the original root compute endpoint's exact hostname separately from its secret. The database client accepts only a direct TLS `verify-full` connection to that hostname: it rejects a child-branch hostname, pooler/proxy or host override, and checks the effective target on startup, every reconnect and before an effect-admission transaction. A secret change alone cannot switch an S02a/S04/S05/S08 revision to a restored child. The normal app and ordinary deploy principals have no Neon API permission to move the pinned compute to another branch; the trusted infrastructure custodian controls that separate permission and may not reassign it before the recovery-capable S29 cutover. Neon permits privileged compute reassociation without changing the hostname, so this pre-S29 pin is **not** a defense against a custodian moving the endpoint; that action is outside the supported pre-S29 recovery path and must be fenced under S29 before it occurs. Human/agent app commands continue to pass through **both** the D2 connection/attempt scope and the prior D3 app role matrix: distinct revocable human identities, viewer, operator and connection admin without implicit operator rights, and scoped noninteractive agent grants for inspect/register/launch/reconcile. The separate application recovery principal has no Fly/Neon deploy or restore authority and sees only minimal recovery metadata; the named infrastructure custodian has technical data reach through deployment and must be explicitly trusted, scoped and audited. The team admin onboards a second teammate, who can inspect from another device while the owner's laptop is off; revoked teammates cannot inspect Pigboard content. Every grant and denial is auditable. App commands must enforce current team and connection authority independently of Neon SQL access. Database access never makes a provider call by itself; the coordinator must commit a D2 effect reservation before any Cursor or Jira send and record its outcome afterward. A failed database transaction is retried only where its idempotency/expected version is proven; a failed provider send is never blindly repeated.

Neon suspends after five minutes of inactivity by default. A quiet database should wake on a legitimate request, with bounded connection timeout and an honest `temporarily unavailable/retry` result. The app must reconnect after suspension and must not use a health query or polling cadence that continuously resets the idle timer by accident. If Neon is unavailable, the app stops new effect admissions and reports shared inspection unavailable rather than using local fallback state. If Fly restarts, the next Machine reads committed state and resumes only proven-safe work; an outstanding reserved send remains uncertain until reconciled.

Current team, role, agent, connection-scope and provider-credential-version changes are recorded as append-only, generation-numbered events on the **live Neon root branch** by authenticated administration commands. A command that changes authority must commit its event and current projection together, then return the committed generation; a partial write never grants access. Every command reads the live projection/generation rather than trusting roles copied into a restored child branch. The root remains current while a historical child branch is created; **never restore the root in place**. Before recovery rebind, the infrastructure custodian verifies the live root's authority journal and current generation against the external recovery manifest's recorded digest. If the root or its journal is missing, corrupted or cannot be independently verified, no role from the restored branch is accepted and the connection remains quarantined until a team administrator re-establishes grants from current external identity evidence. A recovered old role or credential is never a default. Quarantine may use the child branch for run data and the live root for current authority, with a second billable Neon compute endpoint during the drill/cutover. Before effects reopen, the custodian must copy the reconciled run/effect data back to the live root through a reviewed staging-and-transactional-swap procedure that preserves the current authority journal, proves data/effect identity and generation, then restart a reviewed app revision against the root in quarantine. If this copy-back or verification fails, no effects reopen. Thus ordinary effect admission again checks authority and reserves the effect in one root-database transaction; the one-endpoint cost estimate applies only outside the temporary cutover. This is an availability limitation of the low-cost same-project design, not a claim that PITR protects the live authority source from project loss.

The separate private GitHub recovery-control repository is the durable **external marker**, not the source of raw application data. Its write grant is limited to the recovery principal and trusted owner administrator; the app's fine-grained token can only read this repository. A manifest update specifies the expected previous commit SHA and uses a non-force fast-forward ref update, failing on a changed head. Optional branch protection adds defense in depth if available on the account plan; it is not assumed in the pilot cost. Recovery progresses monotonically through `fenced`, `quarantined` and `reopen` for one recovery ID and exact connection/project/branch tuple. The initial `fenced` record names the existing project and recovery point with its new target branch still unbound. After the custodian creates the child branch, the recovery principal uses an expected-head update to bind its exact branch ID and the verified live-root authority generation/digest **before** starting the quarantined revision. Any later authority change requires a refreshed manifest generation before observation or reopen. There is no cross-provider transaction. Before S29, S05 and S08 run only against the pinned original root endpoint; they have no restored-target attachment command. Normal effect admission uses the current root authority projection and D2 reservation in one Postgres transaction, so no recovery manifest is required for those revisions. A restore is unsupported until S29-S31: changing the Fly database secret to a historical child must fail closed, and no privileged endpoint reassociation is allowed as a normal operation. S29 introduces an initial `normal` manifest for the root and deploys a recovery-capable revision that reads it before new effects. From that revision onward, a missing or mismatched manifest, changed authority generation, unavailable repository or failed read closes effect admission; any restored child is quarantine-only until the verified root copy-back and reopen. This staged guard must be tested without retroactively claiming S05 has recovery support. The GitHub commit is a control record, not a substitute for the live Neon authority journal or the physical Fly/provider fence. Verify that the intended GitHub account provides the separate read/write grants, stale-head rejection and an auditable commit history and that ordinary deploy/runtime principals cannot write the manifest before implementation; otherwise D3 remains open. This adds no always-on compute endpoint but does add a control-plane availability dependency that must be shown to humans and agents.

For a restore, the recovery principal records a connection-scoped recovery ID, expected target and recovery point outside Postgres. A named deploy owner suspends all automation and ordinary Fly deploy/Machines credentials for the dedicated Pigboard organization and verifies that only the named infrastructure custodian retains temporary Fly/Neon cutover authority. The application recovery principal has only its separate external connection-scoped marker/reopen grant. They disable normal ingress/auto-start, revoke or disable old Cursor and Jira provider credentials at their source, destroy **all** old Fly app Machines and verify zero old running, starting or overlapping Machines through the Machines API. This is an interlock: the custodian-only deploy grant and revoked provider credentials remain in force from before the zero-Machine check through reopen, so a stale listing alone cannot authorize rebind. An in-flight send at shutdown becomes uncertain, not failed. If exclusive deploy authority, provider revocation or zero old Machines cannot be proved, the rebind stops; changing a Fly secret or config on its own is never a fence. After the recovery principal records the intended target and authorizes the cutover, the infrastructure custodian restores to a new Neon branch and starts a reviewed app revision against that branch in quarantine-only mode. Normal app routes and all effect admissions remain disabled, but a dedicated HTTPS recovery-observation route remains available. It authenticates current operator or explicitly scoped agent identity, enforces the named connection and read-only provider-command allowlist, audits each read, and denies viewer-only, connection-admin-only and recovery-principal access to raw provider content. The route must be tested for these allowed and denied roles before use. The revision/target/recovery ID, deploy-grant audit and zero-old-Machine evidence are checked before provider observation. A drill must race a stale deploy/start request between zero check and rebind and prove it is denied; an unaccounted Machine or successful stale deploy fails closed. Current team and connection grants are first rebuilt from authority outside the restored snapshot. A current-authority connection admin then uses a separate authenticated HTTPS `/recovery/bind-credential` command, available through the same quarantine entrypoint, to bind fresh credentials for the **same original provider identities and connections**. It accepts only that connection admin, denies viewer, operator, agent and recovery principal, never echoes the secret, and stores a new recovery-ID-scoped sealed credential version in the application-managed secret store on the restored Neon target; its encryption key remains in Fly app secrets outside the restored database. Restored older credential versions are inactive and cannot be selected by application commands. This service-held binding does not require the connection admin to have Fly/Neon deploy authority, and the app has no Fly deploy token. The quarantined revision can use the newly bound credential only through tested provider-read commands; all send/effect admissions remain disabled even if the provider token itself has write scope. Test binding authorization, old-version denial and no-send behavior before provider observation. A currently authorized operator (or equivalently scoped agent) uses a noninteractive CLI against that narrowly exposed authenticated recovery-observation route for provider reads in the lost-write window; the recovery principal sees only digests, window and evidence status and cannot bind credentials or read raw content. If vendor credentials cannot be scoped read-only, the quarantined revision must enforce a tested read-only provider-command allowlist, with no effect/send path, while the exclusive deploy and zero-old-Machine fence remains in force. If that boundary or original-identity access cannot be proved, the connection stays quarantined. Only after the lost-write/effect window is fully bounded, all evidence is reconciled, current authority is verified, and the app is reattached in quarantine to the reconciled live root may the connection admin enable newly bound send-capable credentials and the recovery principal use an expected-head manifest update to reopen the named connection and normal app ingress. The active send-capable credential version must also be copied/rebound into that root under the same recovery ID; a child-only credential cannot authorize a root send. Ordinary deployment authority is restored last. The trusted infrastructure custodian and application recovery principal must demonstrate this interlock, role-separated observation-before-reconciliation path, and quarantine command path in a drill; implementation cannot rely on a static boot-time marker alone.

Recovery enumerates every data class and authority change between backup and now: copied Jira source, prepared prompt/provider request, raw provider result, validated evidence/comments, attempt/effect/audit metadata, and sealed provider credential versions plus their key references. It also inspects revoked users/agents, role reductions, rotated credentials, changed Jira/Cursor connection scope, and already-observed or uncertain Cursor/Jira effects. The approved 90-day post-terminal retention applies only to minimal metadata in live rows; copied source, prompts, raw results, evidence and sealed credential versions still need individual audience, duration, rotation and deletion decisions before capture. Credential binding uses per-version data keys wrapped by a versioned Fly-held key; rotation must rewrap active versions before an old wrapping key is retired. Source-side revocation immediately removes an old token’s authority, while app selection rejects any credential version not bound to the current external recovery ID and current connection authority. The deletion policy must retire superseded sealed rows from the live database and restored branches/exports, state when encrypted copies age out of PITR history or any exceptional manual snapshots, and specify key retirement or cryptographic erasure after no authorized copy needs the key. Old copied tokens in backup history must remain source-revoked and unselectable after restore, even before backup expiry. A chosen policy must specify deletion in live rows, PITR history, any exceptional snapshots, restored branches and exports, with exception handling for unresolved effects; a restore drill tests old-version denial and absence of raw-secret disclosure. Restore re-applies current authority before any content read; rotated credentials require a new authorized binding for observation. Unresolved effects or incomplete enumeration keep that connection quarantined. The drill includes a post-backup provider send, revoked member, rotated credential and unknown outcome. Neon branch restore may transiently drop connections; app reconnection preserves effect identity and never resubmits automatically.

## Alternatives and tradeoffs

Fly MPG remains the reviewed option at roughly $49.58/month after 1 October **using the older shared-cpu-4x/1 GB app estimate**; using the selected shared-cpu-1x/1 GB app would lower that illustrative total by about $2.09 if it passes the same load check. MPG includes a primary, replica and ten-day rolling backups in Fly's same-region network. Fly plus SQLite with a single 1 GB Fly volume could have about a $6.84/month app-plus-volume base after October using shared-cpu-1x/1 GB, but adds single-writer volume restore and Postgres-to-SQLite contract work. The [historical Neon Launch design](hosted-fly-neon-launch.md) offers usage-billed capacity and a configurable seven-day within-project restore window. Neon Free is selected for its $0 database cost within quotas and continued Postgres contracts; it trades away that longer recovery window and has hard compute/storage/branch limits. It still introduces cross-provider outage and TLS/reconnection work and has no independent copy for project/account loss. A paid upgrade, added snapshot, SQLite switch or reduced 90-day minimal metadata lifetime would be a new decision, not automatic quota recovery. The normal Neon topology has one compute endpoint; a restored child adds another during a drill/cutover and cannot become send-capable without verified copy-back to the root. Optional Neon read replicas share storage and are not a writable standby like Fly MPG's. The selected option is not ready for use from price alone.

## Author's seven-principle assessment

| Principle | Assessment and gap/tradeoff |
| --- | --- |
| Intuitive | Teammates use shared Pigboard selection/inspection/launch/publish commands without seeing the SQL store. The pilot owner has a one-time credential bootstrap and plain rotation/recovery steps; recovery still needs clear `temporarily unavailable` and quarantine language. |
| Discoverable | Human setup shows approved project names and role-specific commands; agents get structured choices, current-grant status and actionable denial/omission codes. The actual Neon role, Jira metadata and backup controls remain unverified. |
| Delightful experience | One shared Fly service avoids distributing SQL/Jira secrets to every caller, while sleeping Postgres limits idle use. Owner bootstrap adds setup, first request after sleep may be slower, and neither wake nor later control-read failure is masked as completion. |
| Token saver | One scoped choices response and versioned pair result avoid repeated metadata probes. D2/S04 inspection remains bounded, with uncertainty and omitted detail explicit for agents and humans. |
| Secure | The first service holds SQL/Jira secrets and checks current named grants and owner-approved project scope against a migration-created live-root authority row. TLS and the separate root pin reject a child-host secret; a stolen runtime SQL password can alter granted tables directly and must be treated as privileged compromise. The later recovery repository, trusted custodian and physical fence remain separate gates requiring live proof. |
| Agents are equal | A separately named noninteractive principal can use the same selection API with structured input/results and current connection scope, without a browser session. It cannot issue credentials, widen owner scope or inherit Fly deploy authority. |
| Dependable | The first hosted Story must prove secret-safe restart/rollback, bounded store wake/failure and current-grant checks before pair selection. Postgres transactions later preserve D2 reservations; the short Free restore window, quota, deletion and recovery interlock remain unproved. Project/account loss and expired history remain outside the selected policy. |
